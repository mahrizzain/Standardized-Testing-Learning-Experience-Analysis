## Introduction
	Standardized testing is a controversial topic in our society and the education system due to its various implications on students within this multifaceted topic. To get an understanding of what is, according to Arlo Kempf, standardized tests are “measured in relation to other students' performance or by meeting certain criteria [which] are measured by being in a certain percentile”. While having a standardized education system to account for large populations may seem effective, not all agree that having one major systematic approach can account for all students and their idiosyncrasies. In her study, Cristina Pietromonaco explains how “standardized tests were first introduced to schools in the United States in order to find a standard for all students to be compared against”. This incentive was further solidified in 2001 with the enforcement of the “No Child Left Behind Act [which] reshaped standardized testing into an established system where all states were required to give students in selected grade levels standardized tests” (Pietromonaco). This momentous change began controversies within the nation on the impact a standardized system can have on all students and the adequacy of a standardized system. Hence, there has been countless research about the nuances of this topic; however, my curiosity is in an obscure research area: how does standardized testing impact students’ learning experience? This question begins to evaluate standardized testing as a learning system for students on a deeper level by comparing researched factors within various perspectives to build a conclusion rather than glazing over known side effects and creating specific rhetoric; with the answer to this question, we can evaluate the effectiveness of a standardized learning system, how students truly feel, and look at various areas that need further development. 

## Literature Review
To understand what factors of standardized testing impact learning, it is important to analyze current research on this topic to find varying perspectives and controversial factors within them to see which areas of study the paper should focus on to cover major topics. 

Negative Impacts

	To begin, much of the current literature explores varying negative factors that standardized testing can have on how students learn. As Marissa Rapposelli wrote, “Standardized testing continually affects school districts and its students with equity vs. equality”; she goes on to mention various factors that standardized tests fail to acknowledge equity for all students such as differences in curriculums, funding, and mental disabilities (Rapposelli). This idea is important to understand because every student is different and has a unique learning style and should be provided the necessary help tailored to them to help them succeed. For example, despite teachers having students’ IEPs (Individualized Education Programs), which are given to help provide student accommodations, students continually have immense stress if they do not understand the material (Rapposelli). This trend is shown in all grade levels and how high-stakes can cause them to build long-term testing anxiety, believing their worth is based on a grade rather than what they are capable of since the introduction of the No Child Left Behind Act. These factors resulted in students not having a personal connection in their classroom and what they were learning due to the stress of preparing for standardized tests. These implications go beyond primary and secondary education and will continually affect their future learning. This is shown to be a multifaceted problem with many implications to consider. Similarly, in his study, Alan. D Foster sought to see whether high-stakes testing improves the students' knowledge. He collected data from national and state exams and reviewed changes over time to see knowledge improvements. The means of the data were tabulated and plotted and its change over time was determined for the examinations. He is overall able to conclude from his study that the claim that “high-stakes testing will improve the knowledge of students” must be rejected (Foster). Hence, this brings into question the value and use of standardized testing and how it should be reevaluated, which was a common theme among the upcoming sources as they all to some degree see that a standardized testing system is inadequate.

	Furthermore, there are major socioeconomic and racial implications within studies on how standardized testing influences students' learning experiences. For example, Lisa Tunnell wrote in her article that “Socioeconomic class, rather than schooling or grade level, is the biggest predictor of SAT achievement”. This statement suggests that background influences the opportunities students have to succeed in national tests.  The socioeconomic differences are further solidified in Cristina Pietromonaco's study, where she found a major positive correlation between socioeconomic status and the results of standardized testing such as “students from low socioeconomic status were about one year behind on letter sounds and names on testing… there is a test gap among those of different races seen as young as students in kindergarten between white students and black or Hispanic students, because of their socioeconomic status” (Pietromonaco). She further reiterates the many disadvantages that people of minority and low socioeconomic face when compared to caucasian students and more affluent students. These students can not afford the necessities to help them perform on the tests and feel inadequate due to lower performance. 

Additionally, Pietromonaco recognizes alternative methods are the preferred way of learning. She uses the Finnish education system as an example of an implementation of a successful alternative system; Finnish students deal with less pressure due to the lack of standardized testing but still results in high performance since they focus on passionate learning. Similarly, when growing up, Bryan Shaw realized that passion allowed him to have a successful life and even help with his education. He put effort into his passion and improved his school work as he learned to be effortful through passion. He claims that the education system does not implement passion within students, especially due to the use of standardized testing. Due to the lack of passion, students will feel less motivated to learn (Shaw). This supports Rapposelli’s argument as a loss of personal connection within learning via tests and does not motivate students to learn. The use of passion has been proven successful with the Finnish school systems which do not implement many standardized tests and their students have one of the highest academic success rates in the world (Shaw).

Positive Impacts

	While there are a lot of negative factors associated with standardized testing on students' learning experience, there is also evidence to the contrary. For example, in their research paper, Henry L. Roediger III, Adam L. Putnam, and Megan A. Smith largely argue for the indirect and direct benefits of testing, which is extensively supported by their cited research. They also even mention possible negative consequences of testing towards the end of their research; however, they concluded that most consequences are not significant, and if students receive feedback on their exams, there are no significant drawbacks in using tests as a learning tool. Some of the positive effects of standardized tests include helping with retrieving information, identifying gaps in knowledge, and encouraging studying, which improves learning. This conclusion largely contradicts the many negative effects mentioned as seen in Rapposelli and Foster’s studies on standardized tests having a positive effect on knowledge building.

Further, in a presentation by Richard P. Phelps, he showed many positives of standardized testing and nullified much of the current research that displayed the negative effects of standardized testing. He claims that standardized tests are important because the information gained after taking a test can be used to diagnose students and align instructions within the classroom for students, which will benefit learning. This presentation contradicts Rapposelli’s argument since she explains how even though students have IEPs, they students continually have stress if they do not understand the material; it also contradicts Forter’s data and conclusion that he gained from his study. Phelps also claims that tests also provide both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations to individuals such as in demonstrating competence, knowing more, and the consequences of their efforts, which causes students to perform better after each test and learn more. However, the types of motivation that Phelps suggested contradict Shaw's suggested motivation; Phelps suggests that students will be more motivated to learn through test taking while Shaw suggests that passion is the main motivator. 

Finally, there is a large benefit to the investments of standardized testing. This is seen again by Richard P. Phelps who in his study found that “costs of standardized testing are minuscule by comparison with huge potential benefits”. He looked at varying programs that offered to improve results for many standardized tests and concluded that the return outweighed the cost. He also reiterates many of the positive aspects of standardized testing in his presentation, qualifying the socioeconomic arguments made by Tunnell and Pietromonaco. While Phelps’s argument shows that it is worth investing in standardized testing, it does not consider the socioeconomic argument of affordability and the impact it has on minorities and poor citizens. 

Conclusions and Gaps within the Literature

	Current literature seems to have vastly opposing views that contradict each other and does not adequately evaluate the nuances and perspectives within the topic based on the positive and negative impacts of standardized testing. Hence, this makes it difficult to have a stance on what truly seems to be the impact of standardized tests on how students learn by evaluating the factors and perspectives from current research and their methods alone. This is a major gap within the literature which needs to be addressed in this study. Hence, the study is going to mainly focus on the factors mentioned within the literature to answer the research question of how standardized testing impacts students' learning experiences.

## Methodology
Overview

	In order to analyze the research question effectively, questionnaires via electronic survey were conducted with high school students as a means of gathering quantitative data. A questionnaire is “a self-report data collection instrument that is filled out by research participants” (Christensen and Turner 51). They measure participants' opinions and perceptions based on self-reported information in certain populations. To address the gaps within the literature, a questionnaire will be effective as it will provide perspective from the primary source of this topic, students, to see how they truly feel on this topic. This is vastly different from the methods used in the literature, such as analysis of secondary research, which was shown to provide contradiction and inconsistencies. Hence, these primary perspectives from students will show truly the effects of standardized testing on students' learning experiences. 

A quantitative data method was chosen as it is the most effective way to measure and analyze large sets of data, which helps validate the study and puts a value on the students’ perception. However, measuring quantitatively made it difficult to encapsulate the perception of students adequately, which required qualitative methods such as open-ended questions. Hence, I had to find a way to make sure I did not compromise the students’ fullest expression while they went through the survey; that process is explained in the rest of the “Methodology” section on the various steps to encapsulate the students’ fullest expression quantitatively.

Question Development

Initially, when thinking of what types of questions to ask in the survey, I looked at controversial topics that appeared in the literature to address the gaps within them and answer the research question. I implemented these concepts with precisely described questions, which encapsulated the concepts I wanted to relay. There were fifteen of these questions (see Appendix F) to ensure they covered a broad range of topics that were found in the literature. These questions include factors such as the effects of student mental health, motivation on learning, fairness within tests, and general attributions towards tests and the education system. The questions followed a specific order: what the recipients thought about their own experiences, then about their peers, and finally about school administrators/teachers; they were organized from where the participants felt the most to least connected. This specific order allowed the recipient to get a personal connection initially to the study to relay their understanding which they understand the best and are most connected to. Then, they see how they feel about others with similar experiences to themselves, since students' perceptions of how they perceive other students also matter since their peers are also students. Finally, I wanted to see how they feel about the enforcers of this system, with whom the participant is least connected; hence, why it is placed last. I also placed five optional general information questions about their background (see Appendix E) that students could answer at the beginning of the survey. These questions were common close-ended questions, which allowed me to see if certain factors and demographics resulted in certain responses from different backgrounds of students. 

Survey Development

To make this survey quantifiable, I chose close-ended questions using a multichotomous semantic differential scaling technique from one to seven for the rest of the fifteen questions (see Appendix C) (Christensen and Turner 331). A one to seven scale was chosen to represent the recipient's emotion on the topic based on anchors, which are defined in the header of each question page (see Appendix B). This specific one-to-seven scale allowed for a broad range of opinions to be relayed unlike many other surveys commonly found with vague answer choices to pick from or having a very small scale, which makes students' expression too limited. While a larger scale would have provided a more precise response from the recipient, this would have made the responses too sparse and subjective without a standard to go by, causing recipients to possibly feel overstimulated by the excessive number of choices, leading to faulty responses. Hence, the one-to-seven method is optimal as it allows for an adequate range of options to choose from. When I felt it was appropriate to use vague words in a question to relay a simple idea, I made sure to include the vague words in a dictionary (see Appendix B) in the header of each question page. The reader was able to access this dictionary through the survey in case they needed to understand those words or understand key vocabulary. At the beginning of the survey, there is a main header, which explains the purpose and gives a description of the survey (see Appendix A), allowing the recipients to be informed about the survey they are taking so they have a better understanding of it and its purpose. This survey was done using Google Forms, which allowed me to look at responses with effective pre-built options to analyze data such as pie graphs and bar graphs, showing percentages and the number of student responses respectively (see Appendix E and F). Some other calculations and analyses for the questionnaire were also done using Google Sheets, which was connected to Google Forms so that all the data gathered from the response could go into it. This allowed me to get a more in-depth look into specific categories of the survey, which was organized by Google Sheets (see Appendix D).

Distribution

I spread the survey primarily through social media and asked teachers via email to relay the survey to students. This allowed for a broad reach of students to take the survey when distributed via the jnternet, rather than primarily relying on in-person communication. With the use of social media, I got a large number of high school students who were primarily from Irving ISD and the Dallas-Fort Worth area as they were my primary followers on Instagram, the social media of my choice. This allowed me to get a larger set of population than my school alone. This was overall an effective method as it allowed me to get 70 respondents, which is enough to have a statistical impact on this study to validate it.

After gathering the results, there is a correlation in the graphs of each factor. This largely seems conclusive based on the data from the graphs, which follow a consistent pattern from factor to factor. Connection to Literature

In relation to my literature review, the results of each factor mostly are aligned with the negative impact of standardized testing. Many of the negative mental health issues that standardized tests have support Rapposelli and Shaw’s arguments. Students also believed in better methods that supported Pietromonaco and Shaw’s arguments. Finally, students also supported the general narrative of reevaluating the standardized testing system’s adequacy similarly to all who had a negative viewpoint in the literature review. Most students did not support the positive impacts that standardized testing has on the learning experience. Hence, the results of students who did were not enough to justify a conclusion that supported the narratives of those who had a positive viewpoint of standardized testing in the literature review. 

Implications and Limitations

Based on these results and students' opinions, the testing culture needs to be reevaluated. Most students tend to see that standardized tests overall negatively affect how they learn. The introduction of this system of teaching and learning will ruin the future and current generations’ mindsets, learning abilities, and knowledge they gain from the education system. From the “Future” subheading of the “Results” section, students are concerned about how prepared they will be in their future education since they believe they are not prepared for this learning style. As seen in the “Mental Health of Students” subheading of the “Results” section, tests are ruining how students think in general. Students start to build a dishonest mindset and lose their integrity due to them having to cheat due to the testing pressures. This stress and pressure can cause mental health issues in students and build these dishonest behaviors; they also seem to correlate based on this study. Finally, from the “Alternative Methods of Standardized Testing” subheading of the “Results” section, students overall do not see the effectiveness of using a standardized testing method when compared to other learning methods, which again ties back into the future impact of students.

A significant limitation of the results is that there was a low Caucasian response rate. The study could not adequately evaluate if there was a socioeconomic significance that builds a different perception of standardized testing due to the relatively low number of Caucasian respondents in relation to minorities. Hence, a comparison of factors between Caucasians and minorities could not be made, so this study cannot see a correlation between those demographics.
